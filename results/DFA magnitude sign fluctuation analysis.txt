Detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA) is widely used to study magnitude and sign correlations in time series data across diverse fields. In wind speed fluctuations, DFA reveals long-range positive correlations in magnitude sub-series, which weaken with decreasing sampling frequency, while short-range anti-correlations persist in sign sub-series regardless of sampling rates [1]. Similarly, in horizontal oil-water two-phase flow, magnitude and sign decomposition via DFA captures nonlinear and linear dynamics, respectively, enabling identification of flow patterns through scaling exponents [0]. These findings highlight the utility of separating time series into magnitude and sign components to distinguish nonlinear from linear properties.  

In heart rate variability analysis, DFA applied to magnitude and sign series of RRI changes uncovers nonlinear components not detectable by traditional spectral methods. Multifractal DFA shows distinct scaling behaviors during sleep compared to wake states, with magnitude series reflecting critical nonlinear dynamics [3]. For magnetic and electric field variations preceding seismic rupture, DFA identifies long-range correlations in magnitude fluctuations (α ≈ 0.9) and scale-invariant features in electric fields (α ≈ 1), whereas sign spikes exhibit short-term randomness [2]. These studies collectively demonstrate that DFA provides insights into complex systems by quantifying correlations in magnitude and sign, with implications for applications like anemometer selection, clinical diagnostics, and geophysical monitoring.  

Non-stationarity significantly impacts magnitude and sign scaling properties in vertical velocity increments. Stationary data show nonlinear correlations in magnitude and linear correlations in sign, while non-stationary data exhibit enhanced nonlinear behavior in sign series at larger scales, attributed to modulation by large-scale structures [1]. Such findings underscore the importance of accounting for non-stationarity in scaling analysis. Across all studies, DFA’s ability to decompose time series into magnitude and sign components offers a robust framework for characterizing dynamic systems, revealing correlations that traditional methods may miss.
